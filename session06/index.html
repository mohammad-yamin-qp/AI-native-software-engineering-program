How do you turn "feels alike" into math?

The Problem

Plain numbers don't capture meaning
Attempt #1

Give each word an ID number.

king = 1
queen = 2
banana = 3

By this scheme, king and queen are exactly as "close" as king and banana — 1 apart either way. The numbers are just labels. They carry no relationship at all.

Attempt #2 — and the actual math

One-hot vectors: the dot product says zero. Always.

king = [1,0,0,0]
queen = [0,1,0,0]
apple = [0,0,1,0]
king · queen
(1×0)+(0×1)+... = 0
king · apple
(1×0)+(0×0)+... = 0

Two different one-hot words never overlap in a nonzero slot — similarity is forced to 0, no matter how related the words are. We need vectors where distance and direction actually mean something. That's an embedding.

Man → King points the same way as Woman → Queen — a shared "royalty" direction, with gender as a separate direction. Subtract one, add the other, land near a real word.

Worth knowing: this example is a little cherry-picked. Gender analogies like this work unusually well — most other analogy categories perform noticeably worse.

How "close" gets measured

Cosine similarity: the angle, not the ruler.

cos(θ) = (a · b) / (‖a‖ × ‖b‖)

king · queen
cos ≈ 0.94
king · banana
cos ≈ −0.34
(3,−4)·(6,−8)
cos = 1.0 — all negative, identical direction

The sign of an individual number means nothing on its own — only the direction relative to another vector matters. All-negative vectors can be perfectly aligned.

How It's Learned

No human ever labels a single answer
The one sentence that explains all of this

"You shall know a word by the company it keeps."

— J.R. Firth, linguist, 1957

Where the numbers start

Every vector starts as random noise.

king = (0.02, −0.01)
queen = (−0.03, 0.04)
banana = (0.01, 0.02)

At this point king and queen are not similar — just random numbers, unrelated by chance. Zero meaning exists yet. Every bit of meaning comes from what happens next.

Where the "correct answer" comes from

The answer is already sitting in the sentence.

the king raised his sword window of ±1 around "king"

Slide a small window across the sentence. The correct neighbors of king are just whatever words are actually sitting next to it — no person decided that.

In ML terms, "the" and "raised" become king's labels — the answers the model is trying to predict. Normally a human writes labels by hand. Here the label was already in the text. That's what makes this self-supervised, not supervised.

The actual training task

Guess the missing word. Billions of times.

"The ___ raised his sword."

That's the entire game: look at the words around a gap, guess what belongs there, check the real sentence, adjust. One tiny round of this game happens for every word, in every sentence, across the whole training corpus — billions of rounds total.

What one round of the game looks like, in numbers

The model scores every candidate word.

Score = dot product between king's vector and a candidate's vector, turned into probabilities that sum to 1. (Real training checks the true word against a handful of random candidates, not just these two — simplified here to keep the arithmetic visible.)

p(the)
0.501
p(banana)
0.499

Basically a coin flip right now, because the vectors are still near-random from initialization. The real sentence says the answer is "the." That gap gets corrected next.

Pull king closer to the real neighbor, push it slightly away from words that weren't. Repeated billions of times — that's the entire training process. (In practice, only a few random "fake" neighbors are checked each time, not the whole vocabulary — same math, cheaper to run.)

Now this actually lands

Nobody designs the 300 dimensions.

They emerge from millions of these tiny nudges. Modern AI (GPT-style transformers) doesn't run this exact game anymore, but the core idea is unchanged: meaning is a position, learned from context.

Why It Matters

The same trick runs the world
Same trick, everywhere

Search. Recommendations.
Translation. RAG.

  • Search & RAG
    Your question and the right paragraph land near each other in vector space, even without shared words.
  • Recommendations
    Airbnb, Spotify: listings and songs used in similar sessions end up near each other — the same trick as words in similar sentences.
  • Translation
    "Dog" (English) and "Perro" (Spanish) land in the same neighborhood of a shared multilingual meaning-space.
  • Beyond text
    The same idea embeds images, audio, and behavior — anything describable by "context."
